On Living Purposefully

What To Pimp A Butterfly, Kierkegaard, and a failing startup taught me about choosing to live with intention.

Mortal Man ends with a question and no answer. Kendrick reads Tupac a poem, asks what he makes of it, and gets nothing back — just a name said twice into a room that was never there. That ending has been sitting in my head for a week, and it has pulled loose something I have been trying to say for a long time.

It isn’t a passing thought. It’s the culmination of an effort I have been putting in for years without having a name for it. In time it will be passing too — most things are — but I would like it written down first.

Living Purposefully

One of the most poignant lessons anyone comes across is the idea of living a purposeful life. I don’t mean this as a response against any system in particular, and I don’t mean anything revelatory. It is a response to existing: to choosing a path that takes your humanity on a road rather than foraging at its feet. Put another way, we can choose to grow complacent in the work and the environment we are placed in, or we can explore the parts of the world we are given and set ourselves up to cultivate the system we want to see supporting other people on their own journeys.

Mortal Man

The end of Mortal Man is a conversation, and it speaks to exactly where Kendrick Lamar is standing. He has faced reality and is searching for answers about the world to pass on to other people. At his most vulnerable moment his voice changes from what it has been across the rest of the record, and then he is overpowered by the voice of Tupac. What he does in that moment is open himself up, in awe of and in respect for someone who already walked the path he is in the middle of walking, so that he can hear that the world he wants to create is possible. Then he reveals the thoughts he has cultivated with the help of the people around him, in the form of the poem threaded through the whole album, the one that finally explains its title.

The theory in that poem is that a world gets cultivated by fighting your way to the surface; that once you are at the surface you flourish inside a system you built; and that after escaping the system that helped you succeed, you pass your lessons to the next generation — which is what Tupac should have been able to be for Kendrick. The song ends abruptly. Kendrick asks Tupac what he thinks of the theory and gets no response.

There is no response because the conversation is assembled out of an old interview Tupac gave to Mats Nileskär, a Swedish journalist who has spent his career documenting the story of African American artists. Weeks after that interview, Tupac was ambushed and shot five times in the lobby of Quad Recording Studios; he was killed two years later, at the pinnacle of his career, right as he was ready to show the world everything he had learnt. In Mortal Man, Kendrick takes over Nileskär’s side of the conversation, and because he believes he is standing where Tupac was standing then, the edited interview reads less like one person trying to understand the culture of another and more like two intellectuals thinking out loud at each other.

Tupac was where Kendrick had got to at that point in his life: hyper-reflective, ready to show the pimped-up butterfly he was about to become, after sowing his foundations in the opuses he kept making. His death forces Kendrick, who has now come up the same path Tupac walked, to pave the new path for everyone else. He is faced with being the person who lives purposefully after all that hardship. History has repeated itself here without an ending, and we are in the process of watching it develop.

It is a strange challenge when a work this impactful hits you at the right time and in the right place. There is nothing you can do with it except try to wrap your head around it, and that is where I am now. After a week of playing this album nonstop I am faced with myself. I felt I needed to explain myself — not to anyone or for anything, but for the same reason that I need to live a purposeful life.

I am in no position, unlike Kendrick or Tupac or people like them throughout history, to change the system to the degree they could. Even so, living purposefully is a lesson I am still battling with, still learning the nuance and the meaning of. The first challenge of living your own path is recognising where you are and getting yourself into a system that will support your growth. If no such system exists, creating one is a much bigger challenge, and one that most people never take on in their lives. “Firsts” are some of the most impressive people there are; they show you the world that can exist.

The System You Have to Live Inside

The next challenge is existing inside that system well enough to flourish in it. Retaining the inner beauty, the originality, and the core child that you are, while shaping yourself to fit the society that surrounds the people you want to be involved with — that is the challenge, and I have battled against it for as long as I can remember.

Barely a teenager when I moved countries, I spent my time trying to find the childhood I had just left inside the new people and systems in front of me. It was a losing battle, though that is only obvious to me in retrospect. Of course I was not going to find it there. Ever since, I have been swimming for the surface and wading through rushing rivers to work out what I was supposed to have learnt years ago. It made me hate the world I was living in — naturally — because it never showed me anything I wanted to see, and I kept demanding that it would. Even once I had accepted the system for what it was, I had no idea what questions I was supposed to ask, and I became ambivalent about the whole environment I was in.

What that period taught me is how flawed systems are, and it also taught me to be haughty about the first glimpse of reality I ever got. I thought I had cracked the code: systems are just flawed. The inherent flaws of a system are obvious when you study physics, because there the systems don’t claim to be anything more than what they are. That is exactly why they work, and the lesson was planted in me subconsciously through years of describing systems and examining behaviour inside them. Cracking those systems open revealed unnatural behaviour, and the ways the physics of the world actually works. A system teaches you reality by simplifying it, and that is the lesson that planted itself in my mind.

It wasn’t until I briefly read Either/Or by Søren Kierkegaard that the rest of it hit me. I had read a bit of philosophy by then, but I was not ready for Kierkegaard, and I don’t feel that I am now either. Even so, I still believe one core component of that book gets misunderstood. The text was, “…what was needed was someone who could, to everyone’s knowledge climb really deep down into the whole of mediation, mediocrity, and spiritlessness to plant there, for all to see, the explosive either/or.” People mostly attribute this to the meaninglessness of the contradictions that arise from sitting in exact mediocrity, which from that frame is what forces you to decide on either or.

I don’t believe that is necessarily the argument Kierkegaard means to explain. Taking the middle ground on bare connotations is meaningless, yes, but choosing to live between decisions is a choice to explore both sides, whether or not you come out of the exploration with an answer on either. You can choose to like or hate the system you exist inside, but if you are truly there to get the most out of it, you will stay ambivalent and extract meaning from both sides of the coin. There is no fault in the practice of not deciding. The key is recognising that holding a position on something is independent of your ability to choose between decisions when they matter. You don’t need a verdict on a system you both love and hate; it isn’t there to extract an opinion from you, it is there to teach you what you need to learn, and you can have your verdict later. In the interim, you don’t need to create your position — unless you are willing to exist in the limbo of Either/Or for the rest of your life.

Building the System

Systems are designed to support the people who recognise what they are and where their flaws are, because those people know the bounds. When you know the bounds of the system you are designing, you can choose to improve it or start your own from scratch.

To Pimp A Butterfly touches on exactly this. The world Kendrick exists in is one he fought to thrive within. By cocooning himself and working on his art and on himself, he thrived into the artist he is today, and he had to build the system of support that let him do it: creating music groups, developing his own art, using money to buy himself the freedom to keep going. Separating himself from society was partly to improve the art and partly to bring the lessons back to the people. By the end of Mortal Man we have met a man who used those systems to thrive and is now faced with building systems in uncharted territory, while beaten down by every challenge he faced along the way — challenges caused by the people who forced him into the beaten-down world he grew up in. He needed the chronology of the album to get his energy back before facing the next task, which is a much greater one. Being faced with that and continuing forward is impressive, and it is why the album hits as hard as it does, on top of the history of oppression, prejudice, genocide, and injustice behind it. Without people like Tupac to set the example, Kendrick and others might never have got to a world where they could express their art at all. And there will be another generation that gets to move on because of people like Kendrick. They need it the most, because of a history of injustice that most of us could not begin to comprehend, even though the genre of the life lesson is the same.

Where I Am, and Where the World Is

My journey is nowhere near as difficult as other people’s, but I want the clarity and the freedom I have to see my own position to be a means for other people to learn some of these lessons earlier than I did. It is only because I have the freedom to look at where I am without worrying about getting through each day that I get to have these thoughts at all. So these written words should all be taken as respect paid to the people who forged paths without the freedom to be.

Moving to the US was difficult, but a lot of that difficulty was caused by me. I was grappling for the means to find my home while being presented with a reality that wasn’t entirely mine, searching for the world that already existed while learning new customs — a force that showed up in every single interaction and every system in front of me. So what do you do, having not found the world you wanted to be in, but explore. I couldn’t escape the US, so of course I did what I could inside the world I was presented with: new hobbies, new studies, new careers, new sports.

The world was changing fast at the same time, and it felt hard to keep up. Social media and the steady improvement of its algorithms, rapid globalisation, a government permanently on the precipice of having the freedom to focus on the long-term issues it has yet to repair. Then, of course, the advance of AI, attention models and the improvements in LLMs. Everyone is being faced with new systems infiltrating spaces they thought they understood, and everyone is being forced to ask, over and over, how open-minded they actually are. I could recognise how open-minded I needed to be about the US and the environment I found there. I don’t believe most people have been forced to recognise their own level of open-mindedness, and I don’t think most people want to face that challenge.

What History Can Give You

When you are faced with a challenge of that sort, the best first action is to observe the past. The actions of historical figures shape the actions we choose to take on the issues that matter to us. My own journey of understanding was to search for meaning in the Stoics, with occasional detours into other philosophies. I read Plato’s The Republic, Marcus Aurelius’ Meditations, the Tao Te Ching by Lao Tzu, Either/Or by Kierkegaard, and Beyond Good and Evil by Nietzsche (one of my least favourite texts). By far the one with the most profound impact was Meditations, probably for the same reason To Pimp A Butterfly has had such a strong one: it unfurled the chaos of self-reflection that had lain in me for the longest time. My natural tendency has always been to reflect on what I do, but philosophy gave me the means to think about it from a new perspective. What my path needed was someone who was living a meaningful life, a mentor who had decided to take the hard road. What Aurelius taught me — or more accurately showed me — is that choosing to live a life is an option. I had been beaten down by the world, mostly through my own immaturity, slowly forging a path, finishing undergrad, and then meeting reality. Observing that I had not actually been living, I realised I was a step behind and needed to step up: forget the hassles I was causing myself, learn to live a life, and then return the lessons I picked up along the way to anyone ready to help themselves.

Facing an unknown is the common link in all of these positions, and history only bridges the gap of getting to the original starting line. Life for me was wading through unnecessary noise to get back to a starting line that the people of the past had already made clear. Now, faced with all the challenges ahead, the best I can do is regain my energy before taking up the last little bit. History gives us the information that clears the 95th percentile mark — in other words, mastering the basics. I use that number specifically, because it also means most people are not even in a position to use the lessons the people of the past have given us. History is simply not enough for everyone.

Grounding My Own Reality

Seeking to forge my own path, I set about changing how I interacted with the world. I was working at a startup at the time — not the best situation, in short. Because I had been complacent about all the mishaps that came before, I wanted to help fix some of those difficulties. I worked to prove that I should be in a position to make decisions, and then did my best while I was there to make changes.

Another quick lesson I had to learn in that period is that you can give your best effort to your work, and when people won’t take the lesson after considerable effort on your part, you have to let it be and move on. There were changes I managed to make and changes I didn’t, and in the end the negatives outweighed the positives by enough that the arithmetic stopped adding up. Over the following while I cleaned out my workload and divided it between my fellow employees, and talked only briefly about my leaving — enough to give them the space to voice their own concerns without forcing my opinion about the decision onto them. Following its history without me, the startup improved for a short while after the hire I recommended, and then failed for exactly the issues I left over, though partly also because the employees realised they could move on and still forge their own “Berkeley startup story”.

Moving past industry and academia, I also wanted to affect the climbing world. Most people approach the sport with very little intent beyond the experience itself, which is a great thing on its own — the act of experiencing something and being present in it. But you occasionally come across people claiming they are ready to “try hard” or “get strong” with no perceptible effort towards either. It is such a plain and obvious lack of direction, and it is near-on impossible to get anyone out of that headspace. Climbing is really enjoyable, and it gets exponentially more fun the more effort you put into it. Experiencing that first-hand is what drew me into the sport and got me making clear decisions that push my goals forward. But I can’t serve only that purpose either; climbing hard on its own is too egotistical. I want to be at the top level to show people that it is attainable and not as far away as it looks. Every decision I make about my training is an attempt to be better so that other people can see they could do the same. A losing battle nonetheless, because I realise some people need to experience the exact lesson I’m aiming to get across first-hand before it means anything.

The Question Is Why

The key question I see people in these scenarios lacking is “why”. Considering it now, I understand that not everyone is geared to keep asking questions, because it frustrates them: it asks them to ask more of themselves. That is a jarring feeling, but the payoff is incredible. Asking yourself questions, and really judging yourself for who you are, makes you decide what you mean — the either/or of yourself. In the act of figuring yourself out, you keep finding more questions to ask about the world around you, and you get into this endless cycle of figuring out how things are connected, why they’re connected, what relations you haven’t yet thought of, and everything in between. Choosing to critique yourself makes the world more interesting and more beautiful, and in turn makes you a more interesting person.

Artists work this to the core, figuring out what makes them represent their meaning one way rather than another, and it forms the clear separation between a good artist and a bad one. There’s no reason this should be solely in the arts; every sector has its equivalent. I found mine in research, other people find theirs in business, or in law. Missing the importance of what you dedicate yourself towards is the gap most people leave when they try to live with meaning, because dedication only gives you dedication. The world is filled with so many other people — why not live a beautiful life and give that experience to them too? By choosing to live with intention you are actively learning, dedicating yourself to a shared lived experience, and handing down the lessons you learn along the way, voluntarily or involuntarily. Life is already hard enough even while we share our secrets towards improvement.

Losing Intention to the Machine

Lacking that intention in your life becomes critical when you are influenced by the LLM systems that surround us. You end up working towards goals outside your own, developing external preferences, needing constant approval for what you pursue. It becomes too easy to let someone take the reins, so why wouldn’t you — they seem to know a lot. Their judgement becomes the word of approval regardless of whether you had a thought of your own. People get, as they say, “lost in the sauce”, in this day and age. Losing yourself to all the flavours available from one substance. Getting lost in all the information and knowledge without the experience that binds you back to it. Without a reason to be pursuing your goals, they become ghost tasks, missing whatever it is that would make them the best they could be.

When I worked at that startup, the company seemed founded on a passion, and then slowly over time the goal shifted outside of anything that seemed worth pursuing. It felt as if the company was reading ideas off an LLM that sounded feasible but lacked the substance that made the company in the first place. Intention fell apart. Nobody knew what work they were supposed to be doing, because every idea was being pulled apart by AI into some subservient task. Once that process was underway, it became clear how much importance I wanted to place on defining my own future. I pulled away from AI for a while because of this, not because I believed it was a bad thing, but because I needed the clarity to see my own picture.

Re-learning How to Learn

During that time away, I had a major realisation of how much I lacked a passion outside of my hobbies. The job was very much a job, so I treated it as such: still putting in my best effort, but leaving it at that, with no ego or self-worth attached. That gave me space to explore novel research and try out ideas sitting at the edge of what I felt I could approach. It reminded me of my hobbies, pushing my abilities to the edge so that I can learn to deal with harder tasks in the future. A relief, seeing the reason I love my hobbies in areas I had only assumed would make a great career. What a feeling. I was concerned for a while that there wouldn’t be a field that spoke to me the way my hobbies do. So I dived head first, went to several research conferences, and tested out a lot of random and arguably dumb ideas.

Through all of it I was, and still am as of writing this, pursuing my master’s degree. The coursework hit a new high right as this was happening. Because of the workload before it, I had got used to understanding a task broadly and then deferring it to someone, or some AI, else. I had to learn not just to be interested in something, but to re-learn how to learn: to build the passion on the page, inside my own thought process. How to build a brain optimised to your interests.

Note-taking used to be my least favourite activity, probably due to a bias formed around teachers who would talk too fast, erase too fast, and work too fast. If you’re a teacher and you’re reading this, slow down; these kids need to process on their own without you giving them the answer. I still think a lot, though, because I was pushed to work that way. The ideas were floating around in my head but never reaching the page, because I was taught to be quiet and not to write much, since writing was too slow. And because I think a lot, words became my domain.

Taking Algorithms at the time helped enormously. I wrote and wrote and wrote so much. My notebook, which sat at around 2–3% filled for the entirety of the prior quarter, finished at 70% filled after Algorithms. It was in that process that I wrote down everything I thought and analysed how my brain worked around the subject: fine-tuning my questions to develop towards the goal, and thinking logically. Understanding how even a passing dumb question can lead you down a chain of thought that solves all the problems you’ve been having. Recently, the interconnected world of Dark Souls came back to me, and understanding a topic fits that exact theme perfectly.

Writing a lot is not the goal either. It meant I explained every aspect of my thought, and at some point that becomes too much. One time I spent four pages writing up a proof that could have taken a paragraph if I had just worked the problem from a task-first perspective rather than an all-avenues one. My writing had to be more structured and more task-driven, and that translated into my research, developing my writing towards the goals I wanted to achieve: pursuing tasks that meant something rather than dumping text and hoping the answer lives somewhere within it.

In essence, what I’m doing now is a clear example. I never wanted to explain to the casual person what was meant by living meaningfully; I wanted to know that experience and understand what I make of it. Randy Pausch would call that a “head fake”. Writing keeps gaining meaning when you have a purposeful task behind it, because then the writing itself becomes the task, and that becomes its own task — a self-sustaining process. All that is left for us after that is living meaningfully, so that the writing has a medium to express through. Some weird wavy light packet.

Helping Yourself First

So it pushed me to seek out other experiences, other meaningful lives that people have gone through. Some of them you could never experience yourself, but you can still learn from them separately to their true lived experience. There were several impactful stories. Randy Pausch, living with a terminal diagnosis and finding the meaning he sought out while expressing what he wanted for his kids and for everyone who wasn’t terminally ill. D’Angelo, an artistic mind shunted away by a country-sized number of individuals sexualising him, and through the lens of hundreds of years of oppression, then returning to show that nothing can prevent the beauty of his music or of him. And, of course, Kendrick Lamar, who was the final push that got me to write any of this down.

There are so many others, and they all tie to one important concept. None of these people asked anyone else how to build their own purposeful life. They were predisposed to it, and their circumstances made it all the more beautiful. They chose to live a beautiful life because it is in their nature, and it made some of the most meaningful mediums I have experienced and that many others could experience too. Each of them chose to help themselves so that they could help others, and I had to find my own moment for that, however small it may be by comparison. Because you live not only for yourself, but for everyone who interacts with you. Every person who passes you by, for however long in life, experiences some degree of the work you have put into yourself. They will recognise the effort, because it shows in how you interact, what you talk about, and the reason you had for meeting them in the first place. Be willing to share that with others, both to learn from them and to share the beauty they may resonate with.

Shouting into the Void

No matter how much effort you give towards other people, not everyone shares the qualities that give them purpose. Nor can you expect them to; they will only have got however far in life they have had the freedom to achieve. Lots of people will eventually learn to live meaningfully, because our world is designed to support people who believe in their work, but not everyone will. There are places for busy bees and workers, and that lifestyle is meaningful in its own way too. It is not self-serving, nor egocentric, and it does give back to everyone — given a working moral compass, of course. All we can do is show the effort that can be made and watch the effect trickle through the people we meet. We shout into the void and hope the effect echoes.

It is becoming harder for the next generation to exist nowadays, because of how much they have to worry about. Personally, I do believe that we solve problems to create the ability to worry about more problems in the future; if we have more to worry about, we have more problems to solve. However, we are forcing too many problems onto the next generation. AI, global warming, market instability, money-hungry country officials, geopolitical warfare, and so many more. While I believe mine, the next, and the next generation will handle it, there is still a set time limit on some of these. With that much to worry about, living purposefully becomes something completely outside of the picture. Less individuality, fewer important stories needing to be told, less societal development, and more keeping your head above water. It will become so hard to exist that everyone will be attempting to stay alive instead of living, let alone living with meaning.

Which concludes my thoughts on the topic for now. I want to look back on this feeling embarrassed, but contemplative. These are definitely some embarrassing thoughts to share, but someone will resonate with them and learn from them too. Since I started writing this, I have started at my first research gig that has truly interested me, supported by people who believe in the work that I’m doing. The feeling is second to none, and I hope that others can find that feeling too.

It may be a dull ending, but, hey, we’re talking about real life here, not some story.
